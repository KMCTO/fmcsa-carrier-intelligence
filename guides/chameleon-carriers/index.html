Chameleon carriers: what they are and how to check for one

Last updated: November 9, 2026. General information, not legal advice.

A chameleon carrier closes, or is shut down, and then operates again under a new name or number so its history does not follow it. Here is how to screen for the pattern, and how much weight a flag deserves.

What the pattern is

A carrier with a poor safety record, a revoked authority or a string of violations registers again under a new identity. The new registration has a clean-looking record. The people, phone numbers and addresses behind it are often the same. FMCSA has published a method for finding such cases from public registration data.

How FMCSA's published method works

The method comes from FMCSA's Report to Congress, "Implementation of Methodology to Identify Chameleon Carriers". It compares a carrier's registration with others and adds up points for shared identifiers: the company name together with an officer's name, the phone number, the address, and, where available, a D&B number. A carrier is flagged when the match score is at least 1.5 and there is also a motive signal, such as a prior revocation or suspension. Social Security and employer ID numbers are part of the method but are never in public data, so a public-data screen cannot use them.

How much a flag means

  • About 1 carrier in 20 is flagged in our data (4.9% in the September 2026 build).
  • We hand-reviewed 50 flagged clusters. 60% were genuine re-registration or duplicate-registration candidates, 34% were legitimate affiliated fleets under common ownership, and 6% were artifacts of how names and addresses were cleaned up.
  • We cannot measure how many real cases the screen misses, because there is no public list of confirmed cases to measure against.

So a flag is a reason to look closer, not a finding of fraud, and the absence of a flag is not a clearance.

What to do with a flag

  1. Ask the carrier. Common ownership of several fleets is normal and easy to explain.
  2. Look at the carrier's own history: how long it has held authority, any revocation, suspension or reinstatement, and recent changes. A dated carrier selection record collects these in one place.
  3. Compare against carriers you have already removed. The roster check tells you whether an applicant shares an identity cluster with anyone on a list you supply, without naming anyone you did not list.
  4. Write down what you saw and when. The screen's answer changes as FMCSA's records change.

What this service does and does not return

The free screen returns a flag, the size of the carrier's identity cluster, a match score and a count of linked carriers that show a motive signal. It never names another carrier, never shows the shared phone or address, and never gives a score as a recommendation. Naming other businesses from a pattern match would put a label on carriers that may be entirely legitimate.

Identity data refreshes on a slower schedule than the carrier profile, so every response states how many days old its identity data is.

Common questions

What is a chameleon carrier?

A motor carrier that closes, or is shut down, and then operates again under a new name or number so that its safety and enforcement history does not follow it. The new registration often shares people, phone numbers or addresses with the old one.

How does FMCSA identify chameleon carriers?

FMCSA's Report to Congress, Implementation of Methodology to Identify Chameleon Carriers, scores shared identifiers such as company name with officer name, phone and address, and flags a carrier when the match score is at least 1.5 and a motive signal such as a prior revocation or suspension is present.

If a carrier is flagged, is it committing fraud?

Not necessarily. In our hand review of 50 flagged clusters, 60% were genuine re-registration or duplicate-registration candidates, 34% were legitimate affiliated fleets under common ownership, and 6% were data artifacts. A flag means look closer, not that fraud occurred.
